Banning Wholly AI: What "fully AI" actually means for your music

Nov 24, 2026. By PLAIGROUND.

Have you ever wondered what counts as "fully AI" music, and whether your song is about to get deleted?

On November 24, 2026, at 6:09 AM PT (America/Los_Angeles), Tidal posted, "Bye AI music. Starting December 9." A graphic in that post used a harder headline, "AI Music Is Banned," and the post quoted a longer note: "On December 9, we'll begin removing all wholly AI-generated tracks from our catalog, and you'll see them completely gone by the beginning of the year." Variety identified the writer of that thread as Robert Anderson, the Tidal Lead at parent company Block.

Tidal's support page, checked the same morning, is the wording to hold onto. "Starting December 9, 2026," it says, "any wholly AI-generated track that's submitted to Tidal will be rejected and removed from our platform." The page adds that the questions below that notice "reflect our current policy until this change takes effect." The useful question is what Tidal means by "wholly AI-generated," who decides, and what you can show if a human actually made part of the work.

What Tidal says "wholly AI" means

Tidal's AI Policy defines "AI-generated recordings" as "recordings that are wholly generated using generative artificial intelligence." A graphic in the official post says that since July, Tidal has "demonetized and labeled 100% AI generated recordings," and that it is now "banning these tracks from Tidal entirely." The same graphic states the test: "If a distributor tells us a track was created solely using generative AI tools, or if we detect that it was, we'll remove it from Tidal."

The present limit is also in Tidal's own words. "Due to limitations in AI-detection technology, and to avoid false positives, we will only be taking action with respect to wholly AI-generated recordings." It continues, "As reliability of AI-detection technology improves, we will expand actions to recordings that are substantially AI-generated." Asked what happens if a track is "99% AI-generated (or less)," Tidal answers that "some partially AI-generated content may remain unlabeled on Tidal."

In ordinary language, your own vocals, your own lyrics, and your own production, arrangement, or editing are the human making that would mean a recording is not "wholly" or "solely" generated. That is the contrast the policy draws between "wholly" and "partially." It is not a promise that any one of those contributions will keep a song up. Tidal does not publish a checklist of which acts count, or how much is enough. Tidal is the party that "identifies" and "determines" whether a recording is wholly generated. A track can come down if a distributor's metadata says it was created solely with generative tools, or if Tidal's detection says so, and the policy leaves room for later action on "substantially" generated recordings. If you did the work, keep proof of it. Do not treat a light edit as a shield.

If you believe a track was wrongly tagged, the policy points you to an appeal form, in cases where you "did not use AI to create the recording." Until December 9, Tidal still describes labeling recordings it identifies as wholly generated, and making those recordings "not eligible for royalty attribution." The new step is rejection and removal.

The flood Tidal says it is answering

Tidal is pointing at volume. The official graphic says 43% "of all tracks delivered, average of the past 90 days, have received the AI badge." Variety quoted Anderson writing, "43% of tracks distributed to us in the last 90 days have been detected as AI-generated. Something more drastic needs to be done."

The same graphic says 94% "of all AI-badged tracks are attributed to Suno," and that 96% "of all AI-badged tracks got 0 streams in the last 90 days."

On the size of the takedown, the sources do not use one number. The post Tidal quoted said the change "will result in the deletion of 29 million songs, and the estimated rejection of 80-85,000 songs per day." Variety repeated those figures. The graphic in Tidal's own post says 6% "of our entire streaming catalog has an AI badge (that's ~27M tracks)."

Making, accident, and a pile of unheard files

Aristotle used the word techne for making that is guided by a purpose, as opposed to what happens by nature or by accident. A loom, a tape machine, or a model can still serve a purpose a person chose.

Tidal's line can be read that way. The company is not writing a ban on machines as such. It is writing a ban on recordings it treats as no one's making: sound that was generated, submitted, and left with nobody standing behind a choice. "Wholly generated" is a policy phrase for sound nobody meant, or at least sound nobody will claim as their making.

Martin Heidegger described modern technology as a way of revealing the world as "standing reserve": things on tap, ready to be called up, used, and replaced. Music made with super intelligence can be a craft. It can also become a tap. A catalog in which, by Tidal's own graphic, 43% of recent deliveries carry the AI badge, and 96% of those badged tracks had no streams in ninety days, looks less like a scene of listening and more like a warehouse of content on demand. The worry, if you follow Heidegger, is not only that a machine helped. It is that the work never had to be meant for anyone, because the pipe would take it anyway.

Is intention what makes something art? If art requires a maker who meant the work, then a wholly generated file is a product, not a piece, no matter how pretty the chorus is. If art is whatever moves you, then intention is a credit, not the source of the feeling. Many of us live in the middle. We want to be moved, and we also want to know that someone is answerable for what moved us. A label will not settle that argument. It can keep the argument honest.

For creators

  • Label the work honestly as Human, AI-assisted, or Fully AI.
  • Document your human contribution with lyric drafts, vocal stems, project files, and session notes.
  • Keep your prompts and stems as receipts.
  • Credit every collaborator.
  • Tell licensees and distributors up front, and check the metadata before the file is sent.
  • PLAIGROUND sends the AI label to stores and takes no commission to release your music.

If a song moves you to tears, does it matter whether a human meant it?
