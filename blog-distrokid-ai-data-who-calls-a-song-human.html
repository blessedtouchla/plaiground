Who Gets to Call a Song Human? The DistroKid AI Data Fight, Explained

Nov 6, 2026. A score is not a verdict.

The news

Universal Music Group, Sony Music Entertainment and DistroKid told a federal court in an October 2026 filing in Manhattan that they “are working on a resolution” in a dispute over DistroKid’s AI music data. The joint filing was signed October 27 and approved by US District Judge Arun Subramanian on October 28, 2026. The parties agreed to give DistroKid until November 6, 2026 to respond to the labels’ request for a court order. The filing does not say what a resolution would involve or whether DistroKid would hand over any data. (Music Business Worldwide)

Here is what the labels are asking for, according to MBW’s reporting on the filings:

  • The labels served a subpoena on DistroKid in September and asked the court on October 14 to enforce it. DistroKid is not a party to the Suno case; it is a third party being asked for evidence.
  • Their proposed order would require monthly figures, going back to when DistroKid began using a third-party “prediction model” to test tracks for AI generation, showing how many recordings DistroKid delivered to streaming services, how many it tested, and how many the model scored as “likely AI-generated, whether in whole or in part.”
  • They also want “the total streams and royalties” those flagged recordings earned, and, where the model identifies Suno as the actual or likely source, how many recordings are attributed to Suno.
  • They asked for “the name and vendor of the prediction model,” and the basis for statements about false positives DistroKid made in an October 9 response.

Why it matters to the case: UMG and Sony sued Suno in 2024, and Suno argues that training its models on copyrighted recordings is fair use. Fair use analysis looks at whether a use harms the market for the original works, and the labels say DistroKid’s data would show how much AI-generated music is reaching that market. In the labels’ account, DistroKid has argued its AI data has little bearing on whether Suno acted unlawfully. In an October 16 statement to Billboard, DistroKid said it is a third party and is “committed to transparency and will continue to cooperate with the legal process.” (MBW, linked above)

Scale is part of the story. DistroKid says it distributes 30 to 40 percent of “all new music in the world.” (MBW)

Related context: Warner Music Group settled with Suno in November 2025. UMG and Sony filed a second suit against Suno in September 2026. Separately, UMG sued DistroKid in Delaware on September 15; that complaint includes a footnote stating the lawsuit “is not about the distribution of AI-generated music when clearly disclosed as such.” DistroKid says it strongly disputes those allegations. (MBW)

The bigger question: when a model decides what is human

Strip away the legal procedure and something strange is left. A prediction model, run by a vendor most listeners have never heard of, scores a recording as “likely AI.” That score could end up as evidence in one of the largest copyright fights in music. A probability may become evidence in an authorship dispute. It is not itself a legal verdict on authorship or infringement.

Philosophers have been circling this problem for a long time.

Plato and the copy of a copy. In the Republic, Plato distrusted art because it imitates the world, which is itself an imitation of ideal forms. A painting of a bed is two steps from the truth. Critics of generative music make a similar argument: a model trained on recordings produces imitations of imitations. But Plato’s test was never about the tool. It was about distance from the source. A human writing in a well-worn genre is also working from copies. The question becomes how far from the source is too far, and who measures it.

Benjamin and the aura. Walter Benjamin argued in 1935 that mechanical reproduction strips an artwork of its “aura,” the presence of being here, now, made by someone. Recorded music already lost that aura once, and people kept loving it. Generative music raises a sharper version: if there was never a single moment of making, was there ever an aura to lose? A detection model is, in a way, an attempt to measure aura with statistics.

Barthes and the death of the author. Roland Barthes wrote in 1967 that a text’s meaning lives with the reader, not the author’s intentions. On that view, it should not matter how a song was made, only what it does to the listener. Yet the economics of streaming bring the author roaring back to life, because royalties need a name on the check. The more meaning moves to the listener, the more money still flows to whoever is credited.

Kant and judgment. Kant described aesthetic judgment as something that claims universal agreement without being reducible to rules. You cannot prove a song is beautiful by checklist. A classifier is the opposite: pure rule, no taste. When we let it decide what counts as human, we hand a judgment that Kant thought required a person to a process that, by design, has no point of view. And the false positive question in this case shows the stakes: a model can be wrong, and the artist on the other end of that error is real.

None of this settles the lawsuit, and it should not. The labels have a real interest in understanding market harm. DistroKid has a real interest in protecting its artists and its tools. Suno has a real fair use argument to make. But every side is now leaning on a machine’s guess about what humans made.

If a model flags your song as AI, who gets to explain what you actually made?

Practical: how distributors handle AI and AI-assisted tracks right now

Policies differ and change. A tool’s commercial-use terms do not establish copyright protection or guarantee distributor acceptance. Check the distributor’s current official requirements before each release.

What this means in practice:

  1. Know your generator’s terms. A commercial license from a tool is a separate question from whether your distributor accepts the output.
  2. Separate “AI-generated” from “AI-assisted.” Ask how your distributor defines and discloses each role; do not assume one platform’s categories apply everywhere.
  3. Disclose honestly. Disclosure provides context if a detection model flags your track later; it does not guarantee protection from removal, nonpayment or a claim.
  4. Keep your receipts: drafts, stems, lyric notes, session files. They show the human work.
  5. Never imitate a real artist’s voice without permission.

How PLAIGROUND approaches it

At PLAIGROUND we credit each track transparently, marking whether it is human-made, AI-assisted or AI-generated, and what role the tools played. We think disclosure should come from the people who made the music, in plain language, before any model has to guess. Clear credits help listeners know what they are hearing, help distributors and platforms apply their rules correctly, and give artists a record of their own creative choices. It does not change anyone’s royalty math or guarantee copyright protection, but it does put the story of the song in the artist’s hands.
