Nobody asked the singers.

Sep 25, 2026. For decades, technology could bend a voice. It could not take one. Then, almost overnight, it could. General information, not legal advice.

The missing ingredient was permission

Your voice is the one instrument nobody else can buy at a store.

You can copy a guitar tone. You can buy the same synth. The sound that comes out of a throat used to be yours as a technical fact. Machines could make a voice louder, higher, stranger. They could not make you sound like somebody else.

Vocoder and Auto-Tune

Homer Dudley’s 1930s vocoder squeezed speech for phone lines. It still used the voice that went in. Antares shipped Auto-Tune in 1997. Cher’s “Believe” made the glitch famous. Pitch changes. The singer does not.

Vocaloid

Yamaha sold voice libraries as characters, not copies of stars. When a real singer became a product — Gackpoid from Gackt — it was sold under his name. Built in the open.

Soundalikes already lost in court

Midler v. Ford (1988): imitating a distinctive voice to sell a product could violate the singer’s rights even with no recording of hers. Tom Waits won $2.5 million in 1990 over a snack ad. The lesson was on the books before AI.

Licensed samples drew a line

Splice-style packs: the singer records phrases, you license the file. Their FAQ still says you cannot use the creator’s name or likeness — including voice — without express permission, and the sounds cannot be used as AI training data. Use the recording they chose to share. Do not turn the singer into your featured artist.

2020–2023: the machines learn to impersonate

  • Holly Herndon called out “TravisBot,” trained on Travis Scott without consent.
  • so-vits-svc made voice conversion work on a home computer. Train on 20–30 minutes of clean a cappella, or download a model someone else made. RVC followed: good models on 10 minutes or less.
  • The most popular early models were copies of famous singers. From what was publicly reported, those singers were not asked.

April 2023: Heart on My Sleeve

Ghostwriter977 posted AI vocals that sounded like Drake and The Weeknd. About 600,000 Spotify streams, 15 million TikTok views, 275,000 YouTube views before the pull on April 17, per The Guardian. UMG condemned it. Apple, Deezer, TIDAL, Spotify took it down. YouTube cited a UMG copyright claim. The real issue was a voice. The fastest tool was a copyright claim. In 2023 there was no simple national rule that said you cannot clone a singer.

June 24, 2024: the labels go to court

Sony, Universal, Warner sued Suno and Udio for training on recordings without permission. Cases mainly about training, not one cloned singer. Same root: work used to build a product without asking. As of September 2026, Sony and Universal are still suing Suno. Jason Isbell and others have a proposed class action claiming Suno lets users evoke specific identities without consent. Suno disputes that. Some other labels later settled. That is Part 2.

We love new tools. Auto-Tune, vocoders, synths, samples. AI voice can go the same way. The first wave treated singers as raw material. Nobody asked. Nobody credited them. Nobody paid them. That is fixable.
