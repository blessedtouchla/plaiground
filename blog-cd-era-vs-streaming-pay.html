How artists got paid for CDs, and how they get paid for streams

Nov 16, 2026. By PLAIGROUND.

There was a time when getting paid for a song felt like selling a thing.

A compact disc made ownership feel simple. You paid, you took it home, and it was yours to play. Streaming asks a different question: what is a song worth when almost no one owns a copy, and almost everyone can hear it?

The answer is not a mystery, and it is not a promise. It is a change in how the money moves.

A sale used to be a single payment

In the CD era, a physical sale was a one-time payment.

A fan bought a disc. Money entered a chain. Labels took manufacturing, distribution, and retail cuts. What reached the artist depended on the deal, and on whether the label had already recouped what it had spent on the record.

Artists on typical deals often saw a royalty of about 10-25% of the retail or wholesale price, after recoupment. Recoupment means the label recovers its recoupable costs first, from the artist's share. Recording budgets, advances, and similar expenses could absorb a long stretch of that royalty before a check showed up.

A hit CD could still mean meaningful upfront money. A sizable advance and a season in the stores could land as a lump of cash in a way a slow drip of streams does not. It was not automatic, and it was not evenly shared.

A stream is a share of a pool

Streaming works on a different clock.

It is a pro-rata, or streamshare, model. Listeners do not pay per play. They pay a subscription, or they listen on a free, ad-supported tier. Platforms take their cut. The money that remains is split mostly to master rights holders (labels and artists) and a smaller portion to publishing (songwriters and publishers).

Your share is your share of the listening, not a fixed price tag on each play. Spotify, to take one public example, pays rights holders according to streamshare. A rights holder's streams in a market, divided by the total streams in that market, determine that holder's share of that market's pool. Spotify also says it does not pay artists a set per-stream rate, and that what an artist finally receives depends on the agreement with a label or distributor.

Per-stream rates are fractions of a cent. They vary widely by platform, country, and paid versus free tier. Treat any single "rate" you hear as an average at best, not a quote you can bank on.

Two kinds of money can follow one stream, because a song has two sides. The recording (the master) is one pot. The composition (the words and melody) is another. Our guide to song royalties walks through those types. Publishing is not distribution is the short version: stores pay the recording. Societies and mechanical agencies pay the song.

One disc can still outweigh a lot of plays

This is why a merch table still matters.

One CD or vinyl sale often equals thousands of streams in artist revenue for independents. That is not a chart conversion, and it is not a payout forecast. It is a comparison of two very different unit economics. A physical copy is a single, relatively large payment. A stream is a tiny slice of a pool, repeated many times.

Physical sales at shows still have high margins. You paid for the discs or the vinyl. You are standing in front of the person who wants one. There is no retail chain taking its cut in the middle of the room. Manufacturing still costs money, and unsold stock is real. It is simply a place where the math often looks kinder than a play count.

Keep selling the object if people want to hold it. Use streaming for reach. They are not the same job.

Publishing is a separate pot

Do not stop at the recording.

Publishing (mechanical plus performance) is a separate pot. A stream can generate both. Mechanical royalties are for the making and delivery of copies, including many interactive streams. Performance royalties are for the public performance of the composition, including many digital performances.

In the United States, register with The MLC for streaming mechanicals, and with a performing rights organization (a PRO) for performance. The MLC administers the blanket mechanical license for eligible U.S. streaming and download services. It collects those royalties and pays members. A PRO licenses public performances and pays writers and publishers when the song is performed.

These registrations do not replace each other. The MLC is clear that joining it does not take the place of registering with a PRO, or with the U.S. Copyright Office. plai's copyright and publishing helper can help you sort the questions. It is general information, not a filing and not legal advice.

If more than one person wrote the song, get the shares in writing before you release. Money cannot find a writer who was never named.

Owning the master changes the split

The other fork in the road is who owns the recording.

Independent artists who own their masters and use modern distributors keep more of the recording side than they would on traditional label deals. On a typical label deal, the label often owns the master and pays the artist a royalty after recoupment. When you own the master, the recording royalty that the platform pays to the rights holder is yours to keep, minus whatever your distributor or other partners charge.

That is not a claim that independence always pays more. A label might still fund recording, marketing, or a tour you could not fund yourself. It is a claim about the recording share: if you own it, more of that side can stay with you.
