Your Suno song, your rights: what to check before you release

Nov 17, 2026. By PLAIGROUND.

You finished a song. It sounds like you. Maybe you wrote the verses yourself, or kept one generated take that felt like the one. A release asks a harder question than "do I like it?"

It asks: what, exactly, is yours to put out?

John Locke argued that when you mix your labor with the world, something of it becomes yours. A feeling of ownership, though, is not a contract right, and a contract right is not a copyright. Before you send a track to stores, check three layers on purpose: your agreement with Suno, whether a human authored protectable expression, and the public story you will stand behind. A name on a cover does not create a copyright. A registry lookup does not make a song cleared.

Plan

What Suno assigned

Pro or Premier, or free or Basic, at the moment this Output was generated.

Person

What a human made

Lyrics, melody, edits, and performance. Prompts alone are a different question.

Credits

What you will say

An honest label, named writers, and a split sheet that adds to 100.

1. Check your Suno plan, and whether you have a permitted download

Suno's Terms of Service (last revised August 10, 2026; effective September 3, 2026) treat paid and free plans differently. If you were on the Pro or Premier paid tier when you generated the Output from your own Submissions, Suno assigns to you all of its right, title, and interest in that Output, subject to the Terms, including commercial-use limits. If you were on the free or Basic tier, the Terms allow only lawful, personal, and non-commercial use. Suno's help center asks the same practical question: were you subscribed when you made the song? It says a Pro or Premier user is considered the owner and keeps those commercial-use rights even after the subscription ends, while Suno retains ownership of Basic-tier songs.

Pro or Premier

Assignment, with limits

Suno assigns its rights in that Output to you. Commercial use still has to follow the Terms, including a permitted Download.

Free or Basic

Non-commercial use

The Terms allow lawful, personal, non-commercial use. The help center says Suno keeps ownership of those songs.

Download

An approved file

Commercial use needs a permitted Download. Recording or ripping the stream does not count.

Commercial use also requires a permitted Download through a channel Suno actually provides. Recording or ripping the stream does not count, and you may not strip fingerprints, watermarks, or metadata to hide the file's provenance, plan, or download status. Suno says the assignment, and the commercial-use rights in a Download you properly obtained, are perpetual. It also makes no representation or warranty that any copyright will vest in any Output. Platform ownership is a contract with Suno. U.S. copyright is a separate question.

Suno's help pages also discuss using a paid download credit when you remix your own work, including work first made while you were not subscribed. That guidance sits in a remix article, and the Terms still limit Basic-tier Outputs to non-commercial use, so confirm the exact scope in your account and against the current Terms before you rely on it. Stores have their own rules: a platform's refusal does not take back what Suno granted you, but that store decides what you may do there.

2. Then ask what a human actually authored

In the United States, copyright protects original expression created by a human author. The U.S. Copyright Office's March 2023 registration guidance and January 2025 copyrightability report are consistent on the main points. Using AI as a tool to assist human creativity does not, by itself, strip protection from what the human made. Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or to material where a human lacked sufficient control over the expressive elements. That question is decided case by case. Based on generally available technology as the Office described it, prompts alone do not make the user the author of the output. What can be protected, when it is original and perceptible in the work, includes human-authored expression (such as lyrics you wrote), creative selection or arrangement, and creative modifications of AI output.

Suno's help center puts it plainly: contract ownership and copyright are not the same, Output may not be eligible for copyright protection, and if you wrote the lyrics, you own those lyrics.

Before you file anything, make a short list. Who wrote the lyrics? Who created or substantially revised the melody? What did you perform, edit, or arrange?

Whose voice is it: yours, a Suno Voice Model of your own voice, or something that could be mistaken for another living person? Suno's Terms require that a Voice Model resemble your own voice, not someone else's.

If you apply to register a claim, the 2023 guidance says to use the Standard Application, describe the human-authored parts in "Author Created," and exclude more-than-de-minimis AI-generated material. Do not list the AI tool or company as an author. PLAIGROUND's copyright and publishing helper can help you sort those questions; it is general information, not a filing and not legal advice. For when a song may be in a position to be published at all, read Can my AI song be published?

3. Treat remix settings as permission, not a commercial license

If you made a song public, or turned on Allow Remixes, you did not write a blank check.

Remix

Joint, if copyright vests

The Terms call each Remix a joint work, owned jointly and equally, only to the extent copyright vests in that Remix.

Not for sale

Non-commercial

Those Remixes stay lawful, personal, and non-commercial, even on a paid tier and even with a permitted Download.

Your own song

A different help note

A paid download credit may cover a remix of your own original. Confirm that against the current Terms.

Under the Terms, if you let other Suno users remix your Outputs, each Remix is a joint work owned jointly and equally by you and the remixer, to the extent that copyright vests in that Remix. Those Remixes may be used only for lawful, personal, and non-commercial purposes, even on a paid tier and even with a permitted Download. Nothing in the commercial-use section permits commercial use of any Remix. Suno's help center says the same: remixing someone else's song does not make your version eligible to monetize. If you remix your own original, Suno says a paid download credit can make that new version eligible.

The Terms also warn that Output may not be unique, and that the Service may generate the same or similar output for someone else. "It sounds like mine" is not proof of copying. The joint-ownership language is about the resulting Remix, not half of your original, and it is not automatic publishing-split paperwork. If you want collaboration, say so. If you do not, leave remixes off, and keep evidence of your original.

4. Name the self you are releasing, and label the work honestly

Rights are only half of a release. The other half is who steps into the light. You might release as a human artist using AI as a studio tool, as the creator of a named fictional performer, or as one artist world directed by a human. Those are creative choices, not a claim that AI is a legal author. A persona can give a catalog continuity. It cannot invent a human writer, and it should not hide a synthetic vocal as if a living singer were in the booth without saying so.

Human

People wrote it and performed it.

AI-assisted

A human made the key choices, and a tool did a real part.

Fully AI

Generated with little human authorship. Say so.

Be plain about what listeners hear. PLAIGROUND uses three honest labels: Human, AI-assisted, and Fully AI. The credits should match the work. See AI music labeling. If more than one human wrote melody or lyrics, put the shares in writing and have every writer sign before release. A split sheet records the writing shares; it does not decide who owns the recording, and it does not make an AI system a writer.

5. A lookup is research, not clearance

It is wise to look yourself up, and unwise to treat a search result as a green light. PLAIGROUND's research portal can help you start from a name, a store link, an ISRC, or an ISWC, and points you toward public registries to read yourself. It does not certify ownership, clearance, or earnings. A public credit is not a chain of title, and a missing row is not proof that a song is unregistered or that money is owed. Do not treat a track as cleared because a database did or did not return a hit.

A pre-release checklist

Work through this once per song:

  1. 1. Plan at creation. Pro, Premier, or Basic when this Output was generated? Note the date.
  2. 2. Permitted Download. Did you download this master through Suno's approved channel on a plan that allows it?
  3. 3. Commercial-use limits. If you plan to distribute, confirm this file is not a Remix of someone else's Output, and that the current Terms support that use.
  4. 4. Human contributions. Who wrote the lyrics or melody, and what did you edit or perform? Keep dated drafts and prompts.
  5. 5. Voice and likeness. Confirm you have the right to use every voice and face, including your own Voice Model.
  6. 6. Remix settings. Decide whether others may remix this track inside Suno. Public is not permission to monetize.
  7. 7. Credits and label. Name every human writer. Do not list an AI tool as a songwriter. Choose an honest Human / AI-assisted / Fully AI label.
  8. 8. Split sheet. If more than one human wrote it, agree on percentages that add up to 100% and get signatures before release.
  9. 9. Copyright claim, if you file one. Claim the human-authored parts, and disclose and exclude more-than-de-minimis AI-generated material.
  10. 10. Store rules. Read your distributor's terms and the store rules. Then, if you are ready, distribute with the credits and the label already true.

These steps do not promise a particular outcome. They keep you from releasing on a story the paperwork cannot support.

The point of the pause

A release is a statement: this work is mine to share. Locke's instinct still helps, if you let it stay modest. The labor that matters is the human judgment you can point to: the line you wrote, the melody you kept, the edit you made, the credit you stood behind. The tool can be part of the making. It cannot be the author of record, and it cannot sign the split sheet for you.

Check the plan. Check the download. Check what a human made. Then release that, on purpose.

FAQ

If I made a song on Suno Pro or Premier, do I automatically have a U.S. copyright in the whole track?

No. Suno's Terms may assign Suno's rights in that Output to you, but Suno also says it does not represent that copyright will vest. U.S. copyright still turns on human authorship, case by case. Purely AI-generated material is not protected.

I only used a detailed prompt. Is that enough?

The U.S. Copyright Office has said that, given current generally available technology, prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control to make the user the author of the output. Your own lyrics, melody, or creative revisions are a different story. Document what you actually wrote or changed.

Someone remixed my Suno song. Do they own half of my original?

Not under the way the Terms describe this. The joint-and-equal language applies to the Remix itself, and only to the extent copyright vests in it. Those Remixes are limited to lawful, personal, non-commercial use, even on a paid plan. This is not automatic publishing-split registration.

Can I commercially release a Remix of someone else's Suno track if I have a paid plan?

Suno's Terms say commercial use of a Remix is not permitted, and the help center says that if you are not the original creator, your remix is not eligible for monetization. Self-remixes of your own work are treated differently in Suno's help articles. Check the current Terms before you release.

If a registry does not show my song, or does show a similar title, is the track cleared?

No. A lookup is not clearance, and a hit is not an infringement verdict. Use research tools to gather leads, then decide with documents, context, and, when needed, a lawyer.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information for artists, written from public terms and Copyright Office publications. It is not a lawyer's opinion about your song, your contract, or your registration.
