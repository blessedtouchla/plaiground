If you loved a song and then found out a machine made it, what changed?

Nov 22, 2026. By PLAIGROUND.

Draft for Victoria's review. Not published, not scheduled, and not legal advice. Facts were checked on November 22, 2026, at about 4:30 PM PT (America/Los_Angeles), against the two posts listed at the end. Review scores change. Read the sources.

Have you ever wanted to know what happens when a song in a game turns out to have been generated, and nobody told you first?

On November 21, 2026, at 11:00 AM PT (America/Los_Angeles), Dexerto posted that players were "review bombing The Binding of Isaac's DLC after discovering AI-generated songs in a secret GTA 6-inspired mode." The same post said that only 9 percent of "the free DLC's recent Steam reviews" were positive, "giving it an 'Overwhelmingly Negative' rating." Those figures are Dexerto's report as of that post, not a live Steam check. Recent-review scores move.

The post included two images: cover art for The Binding of Isaac Repentance+ Online, and a scene with the word "WASTED" in the style of a Grand Theft Auto death screen. That supports the GTA-inspired description visually, not how the music was made or what any credit said.

Earlier that morning, at 7:52 AM PT (America/Los_Angeles), Edmund McMillen, the game's creator, wrote that he had woken up to a mess, that he "had no idea it featured generated music in the daily run," and that he was "looking into how that happened and getting it removed." He asked people to stay tuned. His wording is "generated music" in "the daily run." Dexerto described "AI-generated songs" in "a secret GTA 6-inspired mode." We should not fold those into one fact we have not seen confirmed.

What these two posts do not say matters just as much. They do not name a tool. They do not describe metadata inside a file. They do not name a composer, a contractor, or a modder. This draft will not add those details.

The hiding is the wound, not only the sound

From what Dexerto reported, players reacted after they discovered the songs were generated. The complaint, as that post frames it, is not that a cue sounded ugly. It is that the discovery came after the fact. People can like a track and still feel misled when the credit, or the silence where a credit should be, does not match the work.

That distinction is worth keeping, including in fairness to McMillen. He said he did not know the generated music was in the daily run, and that he was getting it removed. Looking into how something shipped, and pulling it back, is not the same as setting out to fool an audience. These sources do not give a clear account of how the music got there, or who left a tool unnamed.

Still, the listener's side of the story is real. Trust is part of how we hear. When a name, a credit, or a quiet assumption of human work turns out to be incomplete, the feeling in the song can change even if the waveform did not.

A promise, and a world, behind a track

Kant argued that we should treat people as ends in themselves, not merely as tools for our own aims. Deception fails that test. If generated music is placed in a work without being named, the audience's trust is being used as a means to a feeling the work was not willing to stand beside. That can be true even when the notes are strong, and even when the person who shipped the larger work did not know. The principle is for anyone who hands a file off: do not borrow a human silence, or a human name, to cover a tool you used.

Martin Heidegger wrote that a work of art discloses a world: it opens a way of seeing, rather than only decorating a room. A song with a person willing to stand behind it discloses that person's world, even if the tools are new. A song with no one willing to stand behind it still makes a sound. The harder question is what world, if any, that sound is able to open. Authorship, in that sense, is a promise to the listener: I will be the one you can hold to account for what you just heard.

When that promise is missing, or hidden, the anger is not only about taste. It is about not being treated as someone who deserved to know.

For creators

  • Label the work honestly as Human, AI-assisted, or Fully AI.
  • Keep your prompts and stems as receipts.
  • Credit every collaborator.
  • Tell licensees and collaborators up front.
  • Check the metadata before you hand files off.
