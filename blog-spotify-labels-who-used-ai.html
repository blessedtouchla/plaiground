Spotify Labels Who Used AI, Not Whether a Song Is AI

Nov 4, 2026. Hidden is not the same as gone. A credit is not a verdict.

Spotify says it pulled tens of millions of AI slop and spam tracks from recommendations, and it says its detection has limits. It also labels some AI use. Those are different facts.

Since it started identifying low-quality slop and spam tracks, Spotify says listener complaints about them have gone down. Problem solved? Not quite. Read the details.

What Spotify says it did

In its October 20, 2026 post, Putting Human Artistry First, Spotify says it has taken more than 50 million slop and spam tracks out of recommendations this year. Note the wording. Out of recommendations. Not off the platform. Those tracks can still be there. They just are not being pushed to listeners. Separately, Spotify says it has removed more than 75 million spammy tracks from the service for serious or repeated rule breaks.

On how it identifies them, Spotify's explanation is candid: "No single signal catches slop or spam on its own, so we combine many." Those signals include listener engagement and feedback, upload patterns, metadata and multiple third-party AI detection tools. Then this: "AI detection technology in particular has limitations, and it will only get harder as generative AI evolves."

What Spotify actually labels

Spotify does not put one mark on a song that says the whole track is AI or is not. It labels who used AI, and in which role.

AI Credits are role-level. Spotify's help page says they show when a specific part of a track is generated with AI, and that they apply to individual roles, not the whole track. Lyrics, vocals, instrumental performances, and production are the examples. The October 20 post says artists are submitting tens of thousands of those credits every day, and that listeners can see them in Song Credits inside the Now Playing view on mobile. The help page also says the credits do not appear on artist profiles or as track-level labels. They are optional, and they are in beta with distributors that support them.

AI Persona badges are a different label. The October 20 post says thousands of AI Personas are now labeled, so a listener can tell when an artist's identity is AI-generated. Those personas are excluded from editorial and algorithmic recommendations by default, unless a listener follows one. Spotify says it is currently the only major streaming service that labels when an artist's identity is believed to be AI-generated.

So the accurate line is this: Spotify labels a role, and it labels an AI identity. It does not publish a single yes-or-no stamp on the song.

Three distinctions

1. A role credit is not a verdict on the song. AI Credits answer which part used AI. They do not tell a listener that the track, as a whole, is or is not AI. A persona badge answers a different question: whether the artist identity itself is AI-generated.

2. Hidden is not removed. The tracks taken out of recommendations can remain on the platform. That is a curation decision, not a cleanup. The 75 million figure is the separate count Spotify gives for tracks removed for serious or repeated rule breaks.

3. Using AI to catch AI, when Spotify says the tools have limits. The October 20 post says detection has limitations and will get harder. That means errors. Errors in a recommendation filter do not announce themselves.

The part that should worry real artists

Here is the quiet risk: a human artist with a small, specific, niche audience can look, to an automated system, a lot like something it is trained to distrust. Modest engagement. Unusual upload patterns. Sparse metadata. Experimental sound.

None of that is slop. Much of it is exactly what independent music has always looked like before it finds its people.

When a recommendation system combines signals to decide what deserves to be surfaced, the artists most likely to fall through the cracks are the ones without a big marketing push behind them. You may never get a notification. Your music is still on Spotify. It just stops getting suggested to anyone new.

That is what dependence on an algorithm feels like when the algorithm changes its mind.

Algorithms are weather. Fans are ground.

This is not an anti-Spotify argument. Platforms have a real problem, a flood of low-effort content, and they are going to keep adjusting how they fight it. Some adjustments will help. Some will catch the wrong people. Either way, the rules are written somewhere you do not sit.

The lesson is structural. If your career depends entirely on being recommended, then your career depends on a filter you cannot see, built with tools its owner admits are limited.

The fix is not gaming the algorithm. It is building something the algorithm cannot take away:

  • Fans who know your name. A listener who seeks you out does not need to be recommended to you.
  • A direct line. Email, community, a home base you control. Followers are borrowed. Contacts are owned.
  • Clean, honest metadata. Make your work legible as what it is.
  • A real release rhythm. Consistent, considered releases read differently than bulk uploads, to systems and to people.
  • Clarity about your process. If you use AI as a tool, say which part. A role credit is a place to do that. Hiding the tool is not.

The philosophy underneath

There is a difference between being visible and being known. Visibility is granted by systems. Being known is earned through relationships. One can be switched off by a policy update. The other compounds.

As platforms lean harder on automated filters, the artists who last are the ones a listener can find without a recommendation, and the ones whose credits say what a person did.
