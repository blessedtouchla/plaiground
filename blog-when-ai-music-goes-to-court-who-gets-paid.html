When AI Music Goes to Court, Who Gets Paid?

Nov 6, 2026. Company recovery is not creator pay.

Most AI music lawsuits ask whether a company copied songs without permission. A new filing asks something stranger. On October 30, 2026, Alphabet shareholder Phil Rosen sued Sundar Pichai, Larry Page, Sergey Brin, and other Alphabet directors and officers in Chicago federal court—not to pay artists, but to make company leaders answer to Alphabet itself.

This is a stockholder derivative suit. Rosen sues on behalf of Alphabet, which sits as the “nominal defendant.” If the court later awards damages under the complaint’s prayer for relief, that money would go to Alphabet—not to Rosen personally, and not by default to the musicians whose work the complaint says trained Google’s music models. The complaint requests relief; it does not prove the underlying allegations or establish that Alphabet is entitled to damages.

The 79-page complaint—Rosen v. Pichai et al., No. 1:26-cv-13499 (N.D. Ill.)—is the source for the allegations below. Music Business Worldwide obtained and reported it on November 2.

What the complaint alleges

Shareholder

Phil Rosen sues on behalf of Alphabet. He is not suing to be paid himself.

→

Corporate claim

The complaint says directors breached a duty of loyalty by an alleged plan to skip licenses and pay damages later. That is the plaintiff’s characterization, not a Google admission and not a ruling.

→

Alphabet remedy

Prayer D seeks damages to Alphabet. Prayer E asks for governance reforms, including stronger oversight of copyright compliance.

Rosen’s music claims focus on YouTube and Google’s Lyria models (Lyria, Lyria 2, Lyria 3), plus MuLan and MusicLM. The complaint alleges that Alphabet and Google copied millions of copyrighted recordings, compositions, and lyrics; that Google ingested them without permission or compensation; and that those copies helped build Lyria. It further alleges Google extracted audio for training and “stripped out all copyright information that identified the artists and their copyright notices.”

Those are pleading claims. They are not findings.

Paragraph 178 is easy to misread on a screen. It says the Director Defendants “breached their duty of loyalty” by causing Alphabet to adopt what the plaintiff calls an unlawful plan—one that, in the complaint’s words, sought to avoid licenses “and instead to simply pay damages later when Alphabet was sued.” That is plaintiff’s characterization of an alleged strategy, not an internal Google admission and not a court ruling.

What Rosen asks for is corporate relief. Prayer D seeks an award of damages to Alphabet. Prayer E asks for governance reforms, including stronger oversight of copyright compliance. Even a full win for Rosen would repair the company first. Artists are not the plaintiffs here.

Not creator compensation

A derivative suit is built so a remedy, if any, runs back to the company. Artists are not the plaintiffs. Company recovery is not a payout to the musicians named in the training allegations, and this filing does not promise one.

Training rights and platform licenses remain disputed. Separate musician-facing cases raise their own questions about YouTube’s terms and what they authorize. Those fights are not Rosen’s derivative prayer, and this filing does not resolve them.

Corrective justice, distributive justice

Aristotle distinguished two kinds of justice that still help here. Corrective justice restores a disturbed balance between parties—making whole what was wronged. Distributive justice asks how goods and burdens should be shared across a community.

A derivative suit is built for a corrective move inside the corporation: if directors allegedly harmed Alphabet, the remedy runs back to Alphabet. That can matter—stronger oversight and clearer provenance could still change how models are built. But company recovery is not creator compensation. A corporate win can improve governance without paying the people whose recordings and credits the complaint says were treated as raw material.

None of that proves every training use is theft, or that every musician owns every right in every uploaded track. Ownership, licenses, and fair use are exactly what courts still have to sort out. The Rosen complaint argues one side. It does not settle the moral or legal ledger for artists.

What we keep watching

At PLAIGROUND, we care about the practical layer artists can still control while the law catches up: disclosure when a track is AI-assisted, clear credits, and keeping creative ownership as close to the maker as possible. Those are values, not guarantees of royalties or legal clearance. No tool, including ours, can promise that a court will treat your catalog the way you hope.

So the question this case leaves hanging is not only “who wins?” It is who gets repaired when corporate recovery and creator compensation diverge.

If Alphabet is made whole for how Lyria was trained, and the musicians in that training data are not, has justice been done—or only rearranged?
